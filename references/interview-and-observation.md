# Interview and observation guide

Use this guide when preparing, conducting, or reviewing discovery research.

## Research sequence

1. Set the boundary: one participant, one workflow, one recent occurrence.
2. Ask for a chronological reconstruction.
3. Probe friction, workaround, consequence, and frequency.
4. Inspect an artifact or demonstration when possible.
5. Summarize the workflow back to the participant for correction.
6. Ask permission for a later prototype test.

## Screening an unfamiliar role

Before selecting a single workflow, ask for a recent busy day or typical week:

- What outputs are you responsible for?
- Which task took the most attention during the last busy day?
- Which tasks recur across cases or weeks?
- Where do mistakes, waiting, or rework become visible?
- Which task can you safely show or explain in detail?

Use this only to select a workflow. Then switch to the last concrete occurrence; do not treat broad role descriptions as validated pain.

## Strong questions

- Think about the last time you did this. What started it?
- What did you do first? What happened next?
- Show me how you handled that step.
- What information did you need and where did it come from?
- Where did you pause, switch tools, copy information, or ask someone?
- What made that part difficult?
- What did you do to get around it?
- What happened because of the delay or mistake?
- When was the previous time this happened?
- What have you already tried to improve it?
- Who else is affected or must approve a change?

## Weak questions and repairs

| Weak question | Why it fails | Better question |
|---|---|---|
| Would you use an AI tool for this? | Hypothetical and solution-led | What did you use the last time, and what was still hard? |
| Is this a big problem? | Invites agreement | What happened because it was not solved? |
| How often does this happen? | Recall can be vague | When did it happen last? And the time before that? |
| Do you want feature X? | Tests politeness or imagination | What outcome would feature X help you reach? |
| Why do you do it that way? | Can make people rationalize | Walk me through how you decided at that moment. |

## Observation checklist

Watch for:

- repeated copy and paste
- switching across tools or accounts
- manual reformatting
- searching for missing context
- waiting for another person
- unofficial spreadsheets, notes, or message templates
- duplicate entry and reconciliation
- tacit judgment the participant struggles to explain
- checking and correcting machine output
- exceptions that break the normal process

Do not assume every repetition should be automated. Note variation, judgment, risk, and why the step exists.

## Evidence labels

- **Observed:** directly seen in behavior or an artifact
- **Reported:** stated by the participant
- **Inferred:** researcher interpretation
- **Unknown:** required but not yet established

Preserve exact wording only when recorded accurately. Otherwise paraphrase and label it.

Use “known” only as a container for observed and reported evidence. Keep these separate:

- **Working inference:** a product, causal, feasibility, or AI judgment drawn from evidence
- **Case measurement:** a number from one occurrence, not yet a stable baseline

Do not describe reported recurrence as observed recurrence. Do not call a single event a baseline unless it is explicitly the current comparison point for a named test.

## Progressive evidence requests

Ask for one commitment at a time, starting with the lowest effort that can change the decision:

1. Describe the last occurrence
2. Reconstruct one ambiguous or costly step
3. Show an anonymized example or artifact
4. Permit observation or screen-sharing
5. Try a prototype in real work

Do not request steps 3–5 together in the first exchange.

## Bias checks

- Friendship and politeness can inflate praise.
- Asking about AI primes the participant to find AI-shaped problems.
- Starting from one preferred idea creates confirmation bias.
- A memorable bad incident can distort frequency estimates.
- People omit invisible habits when describing routine work.

Counter these with recent-event reconstruction, observation, disconfirming questions, artifacts, and behavioral commitment.

## Closing summary

Confirm:

- the reconstructed workflow
- the most consequential friction
- what the researcher may retain
- what must remain private
- whether a follow-up test is possible
